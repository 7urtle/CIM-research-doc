Abstract—This article presents an static random-access mem-
ory (SRAM)-based digital compute-in-memory (CIM) compiler
implemented with 3 nm high-κ metal gate (HKMG) FinFET
technology, supporting ﬂexible INT8 and FP16 formats for
weight and activation multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations,
oﬀering conﬁguration ﬂexibility, high accuracy, and improved
area and power eﬃciency. The FP16 digital computing in memory
(DCIM) compiler achieves high arithmetic accuracy by adopt-
ing a multiplication-ﬁrst (MF) FP computation ﬂow, while the
INT8 DCIM compiler provides improved power-performance-
area (PPA). The DCIM compiler engine enables the automatic
generation of ﬂexible CIM macro conﬁgurations based on the
tiling of block-level leaf cells. Our test chip in 3 nm technology
is fully validated and exhibits 124.6 TOPS/W at 0.5 V and 90.2
TOPS/mm2 at 1.1 V for INT8 DCIM, and 28.6 TFLOPS/W at 0.5
V and 17.4 TFLOPS/mm2 at 1.1 V for FP16 DCIM, respectively.
Index Terms—Digital-computing in memory (DCIM), DCIM
compiler, ﬂoating-point (FP), integer, leaf-cell tiling, logic bitcell,
multiplication-ﬁrst, static random-access memory (SRAM).

II. FLOATING POINT PROCESSING
A. Computation Flow
The complexity of FP computation primarily comes from
the exponent alignment, prior to the accumulation. Multiplying
two FP numbers involves two key steps: adding their exponent
values and multiplying mantissa values. Before accumulation,
the exponent values must be aligned by determining the
maximum exponent value among all FP products. During
alignment, the mantissa of each product is shifted by the expo-
nent diﬀerence between its local exponent and the maximum
exponent. These steps introduce signiﬁcant complexity to the
MAC implementation compared to the integer formats.

As shown in the left side, the alignment ﬁrst (AF) approach
aligns mantissa bits according to the maximum exponent
(EMAX) values before multiplying the weights and activations
(XIN). Applying the AF approach eliminates the need for shift
operations related to exponent alignment, thereby allowing FP
mantissa processing to utilize existing INT-based MAC units.
Prior works demonstrated high-energy eﬃciency, TOPS/W,
by employing weight and activation prealignment with oﬀ-
macro digital circuits and software, which eliminates shift
functions related to exponent alignment within their macro
[10],[11], [12]. However, this still requires oﬀ-macro conver-
sions for pre- and postalignment, which introduces additional
overhead at oﬀ-macro, impacting the PPA. Furthermore, in
terms of arithmetic accuracy, because the mantissa alignment
and truncation are performed upfront, errors from both weight
and XIN individually contribute to mantissa product. Accord-
ingly, we observe a visible arithmetic accuracy loss with AF
approach, which had been adopted by several prior designs
[10], [11],[12], [13] using brain ﬂoating (BF) point format
(featuring similar trends as FP format with respect to MF
versus AF). In contrast, the multiply ﬁrst (MF) approach
performs mantissa multiplication before the bit alignment as
shown in the right side. Therefore, it prevents information loss
and preserves arithmetic accuracy for a 22 bit mantissa product
(considering 11 bit mantissa for FP16, including the hidden bit
information).

B. FP16 DCIM Compiler
As illustrated in Fig. 7, the FP16 DCIM supports FP16 and
signed/unsigned INT8 formats for both weight and XIN. In
this design, exponent handling with EXP addition/subtraction,
EMAX search, mantissa shifter, and FP32 output format
converters are newly adopted in the LMAC and GIO. The

FP16 converter handles the hidden bit at the interface. The
weight storage stores the 17 bits by appending one bit of
hidden information. The FP16 × FP16 or INT8 × INT8
parallel MAC operations are handled by the MAC unit with
8704 bits weight (17 bits × 64 input × 8 output channels)
and 1088 bits XIN (17 bits × 64 input channels). The FP
computation ﬂow is divided into two stages. In the ﬁrst stage,
multiplication is performed by adding two diﬀerent exponent
values and multiplying their corresponding mantissa values.
The parallel MAC operation is implemented with a Booth
multiplier to support mantissa alignment at each channel. The
EMAX search module receives 384 bits (6-bit ESUM × 64
input channels) and sends the 6-bit EMAX to LMAC. An EXP
subtraction block computes EXP diﬀerence, while a shifter
block aligns and truncates mantissa to 16 bits based on this
diﬀerence. After conversion to the 2’s compliment format
for accumulation, LMAC outputs a 17-bit product (1 bit-sign
+ 16 bit-data). The GIO includes 18 to 23 bit adders and
FP32 output format converter. In the second stage, the adders
accumulate partial sums (PSUMs) into 23 bits. The output
format converter combines this 23-bit PSUM and 6-bit EMAX
to produce a FP32 output (NOUT). The macro can operate in
FP16 mode or INT8 mode, which is controlled by the FLOATE
signal.
Fig. 8 illustrates the layout ﬂoorplan for the LMAC and
bitcell array. We placed two weight data in vertical direction
to mitigate routing congestion due to the adder tree and EMAX
search. In order to support compiler function, the number of
extra SRAM storage rows is made tunable from 2 to 18. In
the largest macro conﬁguration, weight memory storage is
partitioned into 32 banks. Each bank consists of 18 rows and
272 columns. 272 columns include 17 bits for FP16, 2 input
channels and 8 output channels (i.e., 17 × 2 × 8). A low-

C. Multi-Weight Update per Cycle
Fig. 11 (Top) shows the optimization of memory array.
Running decoder-based generative AI models are highly mem-
ory bounded due to the lack of weight reuse. Prior designs
[7], [8] updated weight data for one input channel per cycle
(17 bits across 8 output channels). This limited bandwidth
leads to computation stalls, which are particularly problematic
for vector–matrix multiplications with no weight reuse. For

instance, in an M × N DCIM macro, updating all weights
takes M cycles, whereas computation requires just one cycle.
To address this issue, we adopt a multi-weight update per cycle

doubling the write bandwidth and halving cycles, enhancing
utilization and throughput with negligible area overhead.

Our DCIM macro with multirow weight storage supports
concurrent weight update and CIM. For a DCIM macro
of size IN CH ×OUT CH with storage row size of
N, it can execute a matrix multiplication of size (M,
IN CH) ×(IN CH, OUT CH) in M cycles. Although updat-
ing all weights stored in N storage rows for all input channels
takes N · IN CH/2 cycles (assuming 2 weight-row update per
cycle), the computation can proceed once one weight set of
size IN CH × OUT CH is updated (which takesIN CH/2

D. Circuit Optimization
As depicted in Fig. 13, a SUM-inversed full-adder (FA) is
introduced to enhance low-power MAC operation. The SUM-
inversed FA inverts only the SUM outputs while keeping a
positive carry path, which ensures faster propagation along the
carry direction. The MAC operation with the SUM-inversed
FA achieves 8% improved power eﬃciency and 7% faster
operation compared to the conventional polarity-inverted FA,
which slows both carry and sum propagations due to the large
fan-out, causing MSB side timing gap and glitches.
E. DCIM Tiling
Fig. 14 illustrates how tiling is supported in the DCIM
compiler. Flexible DCIM macro conﬁgurations are automat-

leaf cells. In order to implement leaf cell tiling, we deﬁned
three conﬁguration parameters. The number of rows and output
channels correspond to the word depth and word width in the
SRAM compilers, respectively. The number of input channels
can be handled as similar to the number of banks in single-
port multibank (SPMB) SRAM design. In the SPMB, reuse
of leaf cells is convenient because global routing can be
shared among the leaf cells in conﬁguration range. On the
other hand, in the DCIM, global adders need extra tie-low
leaf cells to maintain global routing since the number of
input channels is reduced. For instance, when the number of
input channels decreases from 64 to 32, the ﬁnal-stage adder
requires tie-low leaf cells to maintain the global routing, it
incurs slight overhead on the PPA. In addition to the tie-low
cells, peripheral logic (e.g., GIO, WL/XIN DRVs, GCTRL)
involves additional overheads especially for the smaller output
channel conﬁgurations. We set the compiler range for the extra
SRAM storage rows from 2 to 18 with a step size of 2 rows,
the number of output channels from 2 to 8 with a step size of
1 channel, and the number of input channels from 16 to 64
with a step size of 16 channels.

arithmetic accuracy with the MF FP computation ﬂow, which
incorporates a 16-bit mantissa bit width, while the INT8 DCIM
achieves comparable PPA to the rigid INT8 DCIM design
in [8].
V. CONCLUSION
We presented the ﬁrst DCIM compiler oﬀering ﬂexible
conﬁguration range and high-compute precision, and improved
PPA in 3 nm technology. Our INT8 DCIM oﬀers better
energy eﬃciency and the FP16 DCIM compiler oﬀers higher
arithmetic precision by adopting the multiply ﬁrst approach.
Our compiler engine automatically generates ﬂexible macro
conﬁgurations. Our 3 nm test chip is fully validated by using
CIM-BIST/ATPG, and demonstrated 124.6 TOPS/W at 0.5 V
and 90.2 TOPS/mm2 at 1.1 V for INT8 DCIM, and 28.6
TFLOPS/W at 0.5 V and 17.4 TFLOPS/mm2 at 1.1 V for
FP16 DCIM, respectively.

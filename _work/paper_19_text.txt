Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated
exceptional performance in complex artiﬁcial intelligence (AI)
tasks. However, their rapidly increasing parameter sizes lead to
signiﬁcant communication and computational overhead, posing
challenges to the energy eﬃciency (EEF) and memory footprint
of AI processors. Compute-in-memory (CIM) architecture has
emerged as a promising solution to alleviate bandwidth con-
straints and improve EEF. Nonetheless, both integer (INT) and
ﬂoating-point (FP) CIM implementations struggle with the trade-
oﬀbetween accuracy and memory requirement when applied to
LLMs. Outlier-aware quantization (OAQ), which employs low-
precision formats for normal values and retains FP formats for
high-magnitude outliers, has proven eﬀective in matching the
accuracy of full-FP baselines and has become a mainstream
approach for eﬃcient LLM deployment. Therefore, this work
presents OA-CIM, an SRAM-based digital CIM macro that
facilitates element-wise hybrid processing of BF16 outliers and
INT4 normal values. The major contributions are: 1) an LUT-
based multiply-and-accumulate (MAC) circuit design, which
supports eﬃcient FP/INT-compatible (FIC) MAC operations;
2) an XOR-sharing non-maximum exponent gating scheme that
reduces latency and area by bypassing unnecessary exponent
comparisons in FP dataﬂow; and 3) a sparsity-aware readout cir-
cuit with distribution-oﬀset weight encoding (DOWE) to mitigate
the power-intensive charging/discharging process on the bitline.
A 22-nm 512-kB 8T SRAM OA-CIM prototype is fabricated,
which achieves an EEF of 346.6 TOPS/W in INT4 mode and

I. INTRODUCTION
L
ARGE language models (LLMs) have demonstrated
remarkable performance in complex artiﬁcial intelli-
gence (AI) tasks, particularly in natural language processing
and multi-tasking [1], [2], [3], [4]. Unlike conventional
Transformer-based models [5], [6], [7], which typically com-
prise millions to billions of parameters, LLMs scale to
tens or even hundreds of billions. This massive scaling
enables signiﬁcant improvements in generalization capabilities
and long-context understanding. However, it also intensiﬁes
the communication overhead between processors and mem-
ory, which severely impacts memory bandwidth and energy
eﬃciency (EEF). As a result, deploying LLMs in resource-
constrained environments poses signiﬁcant challenges [8],
[9], [10].
Recently, compute-in-memory (CIM) architectures have
emerged as a promising solution for edge acceleration of deep
neural networks (DNNs). By integrating computational logic
directly into memory cells, CIM architectures fundamentally
minimize data movement between processors and memory,
thereby addressing critical on-chip memory bandwidth lim-
itations. To accommodate diverse computational demands,
various integer (INT) and ﬂoating-point (FP) CIM designs
have been developed, demonstrating signiﬁcant improvements
in EEF and parallelism for both INT multiply-and-accumulate
(MAC) and FP-MAC operations [11], [12], [13], [14], [15],
[16], [17], [18]. These advancements position CIM architec-
tures as a robust solution for accelerating both DNN inference
and training tasks.
In the context of LLM inference, it is crucial to assess
whether CIM architectures can still deliver system-level per-
formance improvements. Given that LLM inference comprises

Despite their potential, current CIM architectures are hin-
dered by insuﬃcient memory capacity when applied to LLMs.
This limitation forces frequent weight retrieval from DRAM,
which severely impacts EEF and signiﬁcantly increases infer-
ence latency [19]. Although low-bit quantization techniques
have been proposed to compress DNN models and reduce
data movement [20], they are less eﬀective for LLMs and
often demand signiﬁcant retraining eﬀorts. Recent research
indicates that performance degradation in quantized LLMs
is largely caused by a small fraction of outliers, especially
when the model size exceeds a certain threshold (see Fig. 1)
[21], [22], [23]. To mitigate this, outlier-aware quantization
(OAQ) techniques have been introduced, preserving the FP
format for outliers while quantizing normal values to low-
precision INTs [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]. Among these,
[21] is the ﬁrst to implement a hybrid encoding of outlier and
normal values, striking a balance between model accuracy and
memory footprint. However, implementing outlier-aware (OA)
LLM inference on CIM architectures still faces several critical
challenges.
The ﬁrst challenge is achieving OA computation with high
EEF. As shown in Fig. 2 Challenge1.(a), current mixed-
precision CIM architectures are not inherently optimized for
OA operation. Some designs rely on a single CIM macro that
switches between fully INT or FP modes and thus cannot
handle element-wise, randomly distributed outliers [27], [28],
[29]. Others employ separate INT and FP cores to perform
mixed-precision computation but suﬀer from high hardware
overhead and unbalanced workloads [30], [31]. This highlights
the need for a truly OA-CIM architecture that strikes a balance
between memory footprint, hardware eﬃciency, and infer-
ence accuracy. Furthermore, as illustrated in Challenge1.(b),
traditional low-power MAC strategies are insuﬃcient for OA-
CIM. While previous LUT-based approaches [28], [32] reduce
the number of adders by precomputing sums of adjacent
weights and reusing them across diﬀerent activation bits,
they fail to address the complexities of hybrid outlier-normal
value computation. This is because, in such scenarios, FP
outliers require mantissa alignment before being summed with
INT normal values, leading to increased operand bit-width.
This bit-width expansion directly escalates the number of
required adders, resulting in substantial hardware overhead
that renders LUT-based methods impractical. This limitation
highlights the necessity for specialized MAC solutions in OA-
CIM. Additionally, as shown in Challenge1.(c) of Fig. 2,
integrating FP-MAC operations into the CIM macro introduces
the need for additional circuits to handle exponent addition,
comparison, and subtraction, which are essential for mantissa
alignment. However, existing exponent processing schemes in
FP dataﬂow often suﬀer from long execution cycles due to
time-serial exponent comparisons [33], [34] or incur signif-
icant area and power overheads due to complex alignment
circuits [35]. Therefore, designing an energy-eﬃcient MAC
scheme compatible with both FP and INT modes, along with
optimized exponent processing is essential.
The second challenge arises from the power-intensive read-
out circuit in row-wise CIM architectures, as shown in
Challenge2 of Fig. 2. During the decoding stage, LLM
algorithms typically require extensive weight parameters for
matrix-vector multiplication, exempliﬁed by the generation of
query, key, and value activations (Q/K/V generation) within
attention blocks and the linear layers of feed-forward networks
(FFN). Even when batching and scheduling techniques are
employed to group multiple matrix-vector operations into
matrix-matrix multiplications, LLM inference still exhibits
a lower weight reuse rate compared to convolutional and
matrix-matrix multiplication operations in DNNs. Therefore,
row-wise CIM [36] oﬀers a memory-to-compute ratio better
aligned with the demands of LLMs than crossbar architectures.
However, it incurs frequent bitline charging and discharging,
resulting in high-power consumption.

2) A FP/INT-compatible LUT (FIC-LUT)-based MAC
circuit is introduced, with an XOR-sharing exponent
processing (XOR-SEP) scheme implemented to gate
non-maximum exponent comparisons for improved area
eﬃciency and latency.

II. OA CIM ARCHITECTURE
This section introduces the OA-CIM architecture co-design,
highlighting the outlier-normal aligned quantization (ONAQ)
algorithm and the OA-CIM macro implementation. We open-
source the ONAQ algorithm on GitHub.1

A. Outlier-Normal Aligned Quantization
To reduce the memory footprint and computational over-
head in LLM inference, various OAQ algorithms have been
developed. These algorithms assess the sensitivity of each
weight element, isolating outliers that risk signiﬁcant accuracy
loss even with minor variations. By retaining these outliers
in high precision, OAQ achieves better accuracy than low-bit
quantization. A classic approach is to preserve outliers in BF16
for its wide dynamic range, while quantizing normal values to
INT4, which aligns well with modern memory architectures.
This strategy eﬀectively balances precision for critical outliers
with eﬃciency for the majority of the data. However, as shown
in Fig. 3(b), existing OAQ frameworks often introduce extra
coordinate indices or bitmasks along with complex decoding
logic to locate outliers, along with variable-length storage
format that hinders the EEF of current CIM architectures.
To resolve this limitation, this work proposes ONAQ, an
OAQ framework inspired by [21]. The foundational observa-
tion from [21] is that outlier values play a critical role in
maintaining model accuracy, while the normal values adjacent
to them have relatively lower signiﬁcance. Leveraging this
insight, ONAQ strategically sacriﬁces these low-impact normal

We implement the ONAQ algorithm using the GPTQ iter-
ative method [37], a popular approach in LLM quantization
for its high parallelism and eﬃcient memory access. In each
iteration, outliers are selected based on their sensitivity. To
deﬁne the sensitivity of an element, we ﬁrst establish a baseline
quantization error εWINT4 by quantizing the entire weight matrix
W to INT4 format and computing the L2-norm diﬀerence
between WINT4X and WX, where X is a small set of calibration
inputs. For each element wi,j in the matrix, we preserve it
in BF16 format, zero out its adjacent three elements, and
quantize the remaining weights to INT4, generating a modiﬁed
weight matrix Wi,j. The sensitivity of wi, j is then assessed as
the diﬀerence between (εWINT4)2 and (εWi, j)2, where εWi,j is the
quantization error of Wi, j. An element is classiﬁed as an outlier
if its sensitivity exceeds a threshold sth = r ×τ, where τ is the
Hessian-weighted mean value of the channel variance and r is
a designer-adjustable parameter to control the outlier ratio.
To further reduce hardware overhead and minimize the
memory footprint of outlier indices, in addition to setting a
sensitivity threshold for outlier detection, we imposed con-
straints on the outlier distribution. Speciﬁcally, we enforced a
rule that within a block of b elements, there can be at most
a outliers (in this study, b = 32 and a = 1). For outliers
exceeding this proportion, we rigorously discard them. This
mechanism ensures that, even when multiple elements within a
group of four consecutive values exhibit high quantization loss
sensitivity, the number of outliers remains controlled, thereby
maintaining the robustness and eﬃciency of the ONAQ data
format.
We conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis of
BF16 baseline, INT4 quantization, and ONAQ across multiple
scales of the OPT model. As shown in Table I, on the Wikitext-
2 [38], PTB [39], and C4 [40] datasets, ONAQ demonstrates
accuracy closely matching that of the BF16 baseline, with only
a 0.9% average increase in perplexity. In contrast, INT4-only
quantization exhibits an average accuracy loss of 6.0% relative
to the baseline. Thus, ONAQ achieves a 5.1% improvement

The proposed ONAQ framework primarily focuses on
weight quantization for LLMs, preserving outliers in BF16
while compressing normal weights to INT4. For activation
quantization, our current design employs a uniform INT4
scheme, which has demonstrated excellent performance on

distributions, its generalization capability on more complex
model architectures may require further validation.
B. OA CIM Macro
Fig. 4 illustrates the overall architecture of the OA-CIM
macro, which aligns with the ONAQ algorithm. The OA-
CIM macro consists of 64 512 × 16b CIM arrays. Each CIM
array consists of the write/compute interfaces with word/bitline
drivers, 32 CIM sub-arrays paired with 32 MAC arrays, an
outlier decoder with indices memory, an exponent processing
unit, a product shifter, a channel-wise adder tree, and a
normalization unit. Each CIM sub-array has 16 × 16b CIM
cells, with every 16b representing either 4× INT4 normal
values or 1× BF16 outlier. During computation, weights
and outlier indices are fetched from the CIM array and the
indices memory, respectively. Based on the indices, the outlier
decoder conﬁgures the operation mode of the MAC circuit
and the exponent unit to obtain MAC values (MACV) and
exponent diﬀerences separately. The product shifter then aligns
the MACV according to the exponent diﬀerences and feeds
the result to the channel-wise adder tree, followed by the
normalization unit. Notably, the entire computation process is
executed within one cycle through a fully parallel implemen-
tation. The critical path, starting from weight retrieval in the

As shown in Fig. 6, the BF16 outlier value consists of a
1-bit sign, an 8-bit exponent, and a 7-bit mantissa. In this
work, we adhere to the IEEE-754 standard [41], which implies
an implicitly encoded leading “1” in the mantissa. Thus, the
BF16-outlier value can be represented as v = (−1)S ×2E−127 ×
(1.Man). To align with the application requirements of this
study and maintain consistency with previous FP-CIM archi-
tectures, we exclude support for subnormal BF16 numbers.
Nevertheless, the proposed framework can be extended to
fully IEEE-compliant modes if necessary. In the following, we
denote the combined value of the hidden “1” and mantissa of
weight as MW[7:0] = {1, ManW[7:0]}, which is further divided
into MWH[3:0] and MWL[3:0]. Similarly, for the activation, the
combined value is denoted as MIN[7:0] = {1, ManIN[7:0]},
which is then split into MINH[3:0] and MINL[3:0]. Both
MW[7:0] and MIN[7:0] are sent to the MAC array to perform
multiplication.
The above outlier decoding scheme achieves a 3.82× reduc-
tion in memory usage while taking up only 0.4% and 0.2% of
the total area and power.

To demonstrate the eﬃciency of FIC-LUT, we evaluated the
normalized energy consumption for MAC operations in our
approach against other leading methods at 0.8 V, 130 MHz.
The results show that FIC-LUT achieves energy reductions of
4.76×, 2.64×, 2.45×, and 1.93× compared to the baseline
without LUT integration, as well as Radix4 [42], Radix16

least data truncation, thereby enhancing inference accuracy.

is carried out using the same XOR operation. Based on this

subtractor and comparator, where the XOR gates serve dual
purposes: they function as the bypass arbiter in the comparator,
facilitating the eﬃcient identiﬁcation of maximum exponents,
and act as propagation signals in the full adder, enabling
subtraction operations.
Fig. 9(a) presents the gate-level design of the bit-serial
exponent comparator, utilizing shared XOR gates as a bypass
arbiter. The comparator determines the maximum ESUMn via
a three-phase process. In phase-1, the most signiﬁcant bits
of each ESUMn are fed to a NOR gate to generate EMAX[8].
Phase-2 employs XOR gates to compare ESUMn[8] (n = 0-31)
with EMAX[8], generating Bypassn[7] (n = 0-31) for the next
phase. Phase-3 uses AND gates to gate further comparisons
based on the XOR results. For instance, in Fig. 9(a), both
ESUM1[8] and ESUM31[8] are logic’1, so EMAX[8] = 0. In phase-
2, the XOR gate detects that ESUM0[8] , EMAX[8], thus enabling

to logic’0 before participating in the subsequent NOR-based

each bit of all ESUM values, ultimately yielding EMAX. Notably,
all three phases are implemented within a single cycle, and no
pipeline registers are introduced between them. This design
avoids pipeline bubbles, thereby maintaining high throughput
and computational eﬃciency.

eliminating the need for a large comparator tree without incur-

use of shared XOR gates and a non-maximum exponent gating

leading to further area and energy improvements. Simulated at

FP32 and BF16 (8-bit exponent), and FP64 (11-bit exponent).

the performance advantage decreases as the exponent bit-width
increases, XOR-SEP consistently outperforms the baseline in
accelerating the exponent processing process.

redundant circuits and bypasses unnecessary comparisons,
contributing to lower latency and reduced hardware complex-
